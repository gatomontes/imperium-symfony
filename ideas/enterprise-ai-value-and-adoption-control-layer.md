# Opportunity: Enterprise AI Value and Adoption Control Layer

## Status

Idea for commercial validation. This document does not amend Imperium doctrine, authorize implementation, or place product concerns inside the constitutional runtime.

## Signal

CIO Dive reports that enterprises continue increasing AI investment despite weakly demonstrated financial returns. The article summarizes an Infosys survey of more than 1,000 senior U.S. executives and reports:

- 72% of respondents have successfully scaled fewer than one-quarter of their AI pilots;
- two-thirds struggle to measure AI ROI;
- nearly half lack a centralized KPI framework;
- only about half use a balanced framework for speed, cost, quality, and risk; and
- many organizations feel pressure to deploy AI before establishing its ultimate goals.

The underlying Infosys research states that AI is generating operational value, but enterprises frequently lack the business definitions, measurement systems, governance, and adoption discipline required to identify and defend that value.

Sources:

- Paige Gross, “CIOs are still waiting for AI’s cost savings,” CIO Dive, August 26, 2026: https://www.ciodive.com/news/CIOs-waiting-AI-savings-ROI/828883/
- Infosys, “The AI ROI Gap: Turning Bold AI Investments into Measurable Enterprise Value,” 2026: https://www.infosys.com/newsroom/features/2026/ai-roi-gap-measurable-enterprise-value.html

## Diagnosis

This is not primarily an AI-performance problem.

It is an institutional-coherence problem with models attached.

Many enterprises cannot establish a defensible chain between:

1. a declared business problem;
2. a bounded AI initiative;
3. authorized resources and expenditure;
4. observable execution;
5. attributable outputs;
6. independent examination;
7. an explicit adoption decision;
8. actual operational use; and
9. sustained economic or operational effect.

Without that chain, “AI ROI” becomes an aggregate claim assembled from pilots, subscriptions, employee experimentation, executive enthusiasm, projected labor savings, and selectively reported demonstrations.

A pilot that produces an impressive result is not necessarily a capability the institution should adopt. Adoption does not itself authorize operational execution. Execution does not prove value. These are separate transitions and should remain separately evidenced.

## Commercial opportunity

The market does not merely need more agents.

It needs an enterprise control layer that can answer:

> What did this AI investment produce, what did it cost, was the result actually adopted and used, and what evidence supports the claimed value?

Imperium may provide the experimental machinery beneath several narrower products without requiring the constitutional runtime itself to be sold first.

The immediate commercial proposition is:

> Help CIOs determine which AI initiatives deserve continued investment and produce the evidence required to defend that decision.

The larger proposition is:

> Carry AI from proposed experiment to governed operation and measurable institutional effect.

## Product family

### 1. AI Value Ledger

The AI Value Ledger would connect each AI initiative to:

- its originating business problem;
- intended outcome and baseline;
- accountable owner;
- approved budget and resources;
- model, infrastructure, tool, and labor costs;
- produced artifacts and decisions;
- review and examination evidence;
- adoption disposition;
- actual operational exercise;
- downstream business measurements;
- risk, supervision, incident, and remediation costs; and
- expected-versus-realized value.

It should distinguish outputs from accepted outputs, accepted outputs from adopted capabilities, and adopted capabilities from exercised capabilities.

Candidate integrations include:

- GitHub and source-control platforms;
- Jira and work-management systems;
- model-provider usage APIs;
- cloud billing;
- ERP and accounting systems;
- identity and access management;
- observability platforms; and
- business analytics systems.

Primary buyers:

- CIO;
- CFO;
- AI transformation office;
- internal audit; and
- enterprise architecture.

### 2. Pilot-to-Production Gate

This product would govern the decision that determines whether an AI pilot should survive experimentation.

Candidate dispositions:

- rejected;
- returned for remediation;
- continued as an experiment;
- adopted with limitations;
- adopted for operational preparation;
- retired; or
- suspended pending additional evidence.

Examination gates may include:

- business-case sufficiency;
- defined baseline and measurable targets;
- technical performance;
- security, privacy, and compliance;
- data and model provenance;
- operational ownership;
- integration and recovery readiness;
- economic justification;
- workforce and supervision requirements; and
- expiration or retirement conditions.

A successful demonstration must not silently grant production access, operational adoption, a continuing budget, or authority for follow-up work.

Primary buyers:

- AI Center of Excellence;
- innovation office;
- enterprise architecture;
- security and risk leadership; and
- business-unit sponsors.

### 3. AI Portfolio Command Center

This product would provide a portfolio-level inventory for organizations running many disconnected AI initiatives.

It should identify:

- duplicate pilots addressing the same problem;
- initiatives without owners or success criteria;
- unused model and platform subscriptions;
- overlapping agent capabilities;
- abandoned experiments still consuming resources;
- unapproved or shadow AI usage;
- initiatives without defensible baselines;
- projects whose supervision cost was omitted;
- systems retaining credentials or access beyond the pilot; and
- initiatives whose cost exceeds their measurable benefit.

Commercial description:

> Show every AI initiative, what it costs, who owns it, what it is allowed to do, and whether it is producing measurable value.

This resembles FinOps for enterprise AI, expanded to cover authority, evidence, adoption, operation, and risk.

### 4. Agentic FinOps

A narrower product could measure the complete cost of producing an accepted business outcome.

Cost components may include:

- model calls;
- retries and failed runs;
- tool execution;
- external services;
- infrastructure;
- human review;
- correction and remediation;
- security and compliance;
- operational supervision; and
- incident and recovery cost.

The meaningful unit is not only cost per token. It is cost per accepted outcome, such as:

- cost per reconciled invoice;
- cost per accepted support resolution;
- cost per merged software change;
- cost per reviewed contract;
- cost per completed research dossier; or
- cost per operationally adopted capability.

Primary buyers:

- AI platform teams;
- engineering leadership;
- FinOps;
- CIO and CFO organizations; and
- procurement.

### 5. Governed AI Adoption Registry

This registry would record which AI capabilities the institution has actually adopted.

Each entry should identify:

- approved purpose;
- accountable owner;
- authorized users and business processes;
- models, tools, data, and external services used;
- explicit limitations;
- supervision requirements;
- evidence supporting adoption;
- approval and expiration dates;
- change history;
- incident history;
- continuing measurement obligations; and
- retirement conditions.

The registry prevents a successful demo or informal employee practice from becoming an assumed institutional capability without an accountable adoption decision.

Primary buyers:

- governance, risk, and compliance;
- internal audit;
- regulated enterprises;
- enterprise architecture; and
- AI operations.

### 6. AI ROI Readiness Assessment

This is the recommended service-led entry product and likely the fastest route to revenue.

The assessment would produce:

- an inventory of current AI initiatives;
- a measurement-readiness score;
- missing ownership and objective findings;
- baseline and KPI gaps;
- cost-attribution findings;
- scale-readiness findings;
- governance and authority gaps;
- adoption and operational-use gaps;
- duplicated or orphaned initiatives; and
- a prioritized remediation roadmap.

This can begin as a fixed-price examination supported by software. Repeated assessments can reveal the common structures required for a scalable platform.

Primary buyer:

- an organization already spending materially on AI but unable to defend the expenditure or decide which pilots should continue.

## Recommended commercial sequence

1. **AI ROI Readiness Assessment**
   - enter the organization through a bounded, legible problem;
   - inventory initiatives and expose missing evidence.

2. **AI Portfolio Command Center**
   - establish continuing visibility over ownership, costs, permissions, and status.

3. **Pilot-to-Production Gate**
   - govern which initiatives progress and under what limitations.

4. **AI Value Ledger**
   - connect cost, execution, adoption, use, and attributable effects.

5. **Governed AI Adoption Registry**
   - preserve the institution’s authorized capability inventory.

6. **Governed execution runtime**
   - support increasingly autonomous work through bounded authority and evidence.

The products can be offered separately, but together they form an enterprise AI value and adoption control layer.

## Recommended first wedge

Begin with the AI ROI Readiness Assessment.

Reasons:

- the pain is already acknowledged at CIO and CFO level;
- it does not require the customer to replace its current AI platforms;
- it can inspect existing pilots regardless of model provider;
- it produces an immediately understandable deliverable;
- it creates the data required to validate later products;
- it exposes buyers, integrations, and recurring governance needs; and
- it avoids selling a large runtime before the commercial surface is proven.

Suggested positioning:

> We determine which AI initiatives deserve continued investment, which are ready for operational adoption, and which claims of value can actually be defended.

## Minimum evidence model

For each initiative, record at least:

- initiative identity and owner;
- originating problem;
- baseline condition;
- intended outcome;
- success and failure criteria;
- budget and actual cost;
- data, models, tools, credentials, and systems used;
- granted authority and important exclusions;
- execution history;
- produced outputs;
- examination findings;
- adoption disposition;
- operational-use evidence;
- observed business effect;
- attribution confidence;
- risk and remediation cost;
- continuing obligations; and
- suspension, expiration, or retirement state.

## ROI surface

Measure:

- total initiative cost;
- cost per accepted outcome;
- cost per adopted capability;
- cost per successful operational exercise;
- pilot-to-adoption conversion;
- time from proposal to disposition;
- time from adoption to measurable effect;
- avoided licensing, labor, or consulting cost;
- speed-to-market change;
- quality and defect change;
- supervision and review burden;
- prevented unauthorized actions;
- rejected or retired duplicate initiatives;
- incident and remediation cost;
- expected-versus-realized value; and
- confidence in causal attribution.

The product must not convert weak correlations into confident savings claims. Attribution confidence should be explicit.

## Differentiation

Avoid:

> A dashboard for tracking AI projects and token spending.

Prefer:

> An evidence system that connects an AI business case to bounded execution, accountable adoption, actual operational use, and measurable institutional effect.

Avoid:

> We help enterprises scale successful pilots.

Prefer:

> We help enterprises determine which pilots deserve to scale, what authority scaling requires, and what evidence must survive the decision.

## Risks

Commercial validation should test:

- whether enterprises will expose sufficient cost and outcome data;
- whether buyers already consider existing FinOps or portfolio tools adequate;
- how much causal attribution can be automated;
- whether the assessment can avoid becoming generic consulting;
- which integrations are required for credible evidence;
- whether governance buyers and budget owners can share one product;
- whether customers will accept explicit uncertainty in ROI claims; and
- whether the product can remain provider-neutral.

## Next validation step

Interview one CIO, CFO, AI-program owner, or enterprise architect responsible for several AI pilots.

Ask them to choose one initiative and produce:

- the original business case;
- accountable owner;
- approved and actual cost;
- baseline;
- intended KPI;
- execution evidence;
- adoption decision;
- proof of operational use;
- observed business effect;
- attribution method;
- supervision and remediation cost; and
- current continue, change, suspend, or retire decision.

Every item they cannot produce is potential product surface.

The validation question is not whether they want another AI dashboard.

It is whether they will pay for a defensible answer to:

> Which AI initiatives are producing value, which deserve further authority and investment, and how do we prove it?
